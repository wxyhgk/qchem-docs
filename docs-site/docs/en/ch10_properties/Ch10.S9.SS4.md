## 10.9.4 Transition-Optimized Shifted Hermite Theory

(July 4, 2026)

So far, every aspect of solving the nuclear wave equation has been
considered, except the wave function. Since Schrödinger proposed his
equation, the nuclear wave function has traditionally be expressed in terms
of Hermite functions, which are designed for the harmonic oscillator case.
Recently a modified representation has been presented.[[829]](../bib_references/bib.md#bib865)
 To
demonstrate how this approximation works, we start with a simple example. For
a diatomic molecule, the Hamiltonian with up to quartic derivatives can be
written as

<a id="Ch10.E105"></a>
$$
\hat{H}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial q^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}{\omega}^{2}q^{2}+\eta_{iii}q^{3}+\eta_{iiii}q^{4} \tag{10.105}
$$

and the wave function is expressed as in Eq. ([10.96](Ch10.S9.SS2.md#eq:wave_function)). Now, if
we shift the center of the wave function by $\sigma$, which is equivalent to a
translation of the normal coordinate $q$, the shape will still remain the same,
but the anharmonic correction can now be incorporated into the wave function.
For a ground vibrational state, the wave function is written as

<a id="Ch10.E106"></a>
$$
\phi^{(0)}={\left(\frac{\omega}{\pi}\right)}^{\frac{1}{4}}e^{-\frac{\omega}{2}{\left(q-\sigma\right)}^{2}} \tag{10.106}
$$

Similarly, for the first excited vibrational state, we have

<a id="Ch10.E107"></a>
$$
\phi^{(1)}={\left(\frac{4{\omega}^{3}}{\pi}\right)}^{\frac{1}{4}}\left(q-\sigma\right)e^{\frac{\omega}{2}{\left(q-\sigma\right)}^{2}} \tag{10.107}
$$

Therefore, the energy difference between the first vibrational excited state
and the ground state is

<a id="eq:di-TOSH"></a>
$$
\Delta E_{\mathrm{TOSH}}=\omega+\frac{\eta_{iiii}}{8{\omega}^{2}}+\frac{\eta_{iii}\sigma}{2\omega}+\frac{\eta_{iiii}{\sigma}^{2}}{4\omega} \tag{10.108}
$$

This is the fundamental vibrational frequency from first-order perturbation
theory.

Meanwhile, We know from the first-order perturbation theory with an ordinary
wave function within a QFF PES, the energy is

<a id="Ch10.E109"></a>
$$
\Delta E_{\mathrm{VPT}1}=\omega+\frac{\eta_{iiii}}{8{\omega}^{2}} \tag{10.109}
$$

The differences between these two wave functions are the two extra terms arising
from the shift in Eq. ([10.108](#eq:di-TOSH)). To determine the shift, we compare
the energy with that from second-order perturbation theory:

<a id="Ch10.E110"></a>
$$
\Delta E_{\mathrm{VPT}2}=\omega+\frac{\eta_{iiii}}{8{\omega}^{2}}-\frac{5{\eta_{iii}}^{2}}{24{\omega}^{4}} \tag{10.110}
$$

Since $\sigma$ is a very small quantity compared with the other variables, we
ignore the contribution of $\sigma^{2}$ and compare $\Delta E_{\mathrm{TOSH}}$ with
$\Delta E_{\mathrm{VPT}2}$, which yields an initial guess for $\sigma$:

<a id="Ch10.E111"></a>
$$
\sigma=-\frac{5}{12}\frac{\eta_{iii}}{\omega^{3}} \tag{10.111}
$$

Because the only difference between this approach and the ordinary wave function
is the shift in the normal coordinate, we call it “transition-optimized
shifted Hermite” (TOSH) functions.[[829]](../bib_references/bib.md#bib865)
 This approximation gives
second-order accuracy at only first-order cost.

For polyatomic molecules, we consider Eq. ([10.108](#eq:di-TOSH)), and propose
that the energy of the $i$th mode be expressed as:

<a id="Ch10.E112"></a>
$$
\Delta E_{i}^{\mathrm{TOSH}}=\omega_{i}+\frac{1}{8\omega_{i}}\sum_{j}\frac{\eta_{iijj}}{\omega_{j}}+\frac{1}{2\omega_{i}}\sum_{j}\eta_{iij}\sigma_{ij}+\frac{1}{4\omega_{i}}\sum_{j,k}\eta_{iijk}\sigma_{ij}\sigma_{ik} \tag{10.112}
$$

Following the same approach as for the diatomic case, by comparing this with
the energy from second-order perturbation theory, we obtain the shift as

<a id="Ch10.E113"></a>
$$
\sigma_{ij}=\frac{(\delta_{ij}-2)(\omega_{i}+\omega_{j})\eta_{iij}}{4\omega_{i}\omega_{j}^{2}(2\omega_{i}+\omega_{j})}-\sum_{k}\frac{\eta_{kkj}}{4\omega_{k}\omega_{j}^{2}} \tag{10.113}
$$
